\begin{abstract}
Industrial anomaly detection is commonly reported on static image sets, yet
inspection systems receive samples over time and must balance accuracy,
calibration, latency, and changing stream conditions. We present ZIAD, a
streaming protocol for zero-shot industrial anomaly detection evaluation that
also includes training-light reference detectors. Rather than proposing a new
detector, ZIAD turns static industrial anomaly benchmarks into controlled
i.i.d. and bursty streams and evaluates methods along accuracy, calibration,
latency, contamination, and stream-order axes while recording memory
configuration for later protocol slices. We instantiate the protocol on MVTec AD
and VisA with four
representative baselines: PatchCore as a strong training-light reference, and
WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP as CLIP-style baselines. The resulting
comparison yields two key findings: the four frozen baselines exhibit near-zero
stream-order sensitivity, confirming that current IAD detectors score each
image independently regardless of arrival pattern, while a post-hoc
sliding-window positive control applied to the same frozen scores produces
statistically significant non-zero $\Delta$B-I on multiple (baseline, dataset)
cells. This shows that the stream-order axis is measurable when a scoring rule
uses previous stream items. Accuracy, calibration, latency, and contamination
diagnostics rank methods differently across datasets, illustrating that AUROC
alone is insufficient for operational evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Industrial anomaly detection is often evaluated on static test sets, but
practical inspection systems receive images over time. This mismatch is
especially important for zero-shot and training-light anomaly detection, where
category-specific training may be reduced or avoided, but where deployment
still depends on latency, score calibration, stream order, anomaly prevalence,
and contamination. Memory configuration is implemented and recorded by the
protocol, but the focused slice in this paper does not claim a memory-policy
comparison. PatchCore provides a strong training-light reference point, while
WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP represent CLIP-style zero-shot baselines. A
single offline AUROC number does not expose these operational dimensions.

This paper uses ZIAD as shorthand for zero-shot industrial anomaly detection
evaluation under streaming conditions. It positions ZIAD as an evaluation
protocol and analysis framework, not as a new model. The protocol asks whether
common zero-shot and training-light industrial anomaly detectors preserve their
behavior when test samples arrive as streams. It also records whether a method
is fast enough for repeated inspection use, whether its scores are calibrated,
and whether i.i.d. versus bursty anomaly arrivals change the resulting metrics.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a streaming industrial anomaly detection protocol for zero-shot and
training-light detectors that converts static category benchmarks into
controlled i.i.d. and bursty streams;
\item a category-sharded execution design that makes long evaluations
restartable while preserving dataset and label integrity;
\item a common baseline interface and multi-metric comparison across MVTec AD
and VisA with PatchCore, WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP; and
\item an accuracy--latency--calibration analysis that exposes operational
trade-offs hidden by static AUROC-only reporting; and
\item a two-sided stream-order finding: the four frozen baselines score
images independently of arrival pattern, while a thin post-hoc OnlineWindow
wrapper applied to the same scores produces statistically significant non-zero
$\Delta$B-I, demonstrating that the protocol's stream-order axis is empirically
measurable rather than trivially zero.
\end{itemize}

The paper reports a focused evaluation slice and uses it to study protocol
behavior rather than to make new best-detector claims.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Industrial anomaly detection and its benchmarks.}
Industrial anomaly detection benchmarks have typically measured whether a method
can separate normal product images from defective ones on static category-level
test sets. MVTec AD~\cite{Bergmann2019MVTec} and VisA~\cite{Zou2022VisA} served
as central benchmarks, and training-light methods such as
PatchCore~\cite{Roth2022PatchCore} became common reference points. Recent studies
argued that a single AUROC number was insufficient: standardized suites such as
IM-IAD~\cite{Xie2024IMIAD} and critical analyses of the academia--industry
gap~\cite{Baitieva2025Beyond} reported multiple metrics and stressed deployment
realism. However, even these comprehensive multi-metric studies evaluated on
static, unordered test sets and did not report score calibration or distinguish
i.i.d. from bursty arrivals. ZIAD instead reorders the same datasets into streams
and adds calibration and arrival pattern as first-class evaluation axes, making
assumptions that IM-IAD held fixed into observable, configurable protocol
dimensions.

\paragraph{Zero-shot anomaly detection and CLIP-style baselines.}
CLIP-style detectors reduced category-specific supervision with
vision-language representations or prompt-driven scoring. WinCLIP,
AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP are used here as representative
\cite{Jeong2023WinCLIP,Zhou2024AnomalyCLIP,He2025RareCLIP}. Notably, recent
industrial benchmarks explicitly excluded such zero-shot vision-language models
from their scope~\cite{Baitieva2025Beyond}, leaving their streaming behavior
unexamined. This paper does not modify their architectures; it evaluates how
their outputs behave under streaming conditions. PatchCore is included separately
as a training-light reference rather than a CLIP-based method.

\paragraph{Streaming and online anomaly evaluation.}
Streaming evaluation emphasizes sample order, distribution shift, bursty arrival,
contamination, and resume-safe execution. General frameworks for time-series and
tabular streams reported that no single detector dominates across stream
conditions~\cite{Calikus2020NoFreeLunch,Chandola2009AnomalyDetection,Bifet2010MOA}.
These concerns were far less visible in static industrial image leaderboards. ZIAD
ports this streaming view to industrial images, making i.i.d. and bursty arrivals
comparable under the same baselines and metrics.

\paragraph{Calibration and reliability.}
Anomaly scores often drive thresholds or alarms, so ranking quality alone is
insufficient. Calibration metrics such as ECE~\cite{Guo2017Calibration} quantified
whether scores are interpretable across streams, categories, and contamination
settings. Yet industrial AD studies rarely reported calibration alongside accuracy
and latency. ZIAD therefore reports ECE together with AUROC, AUPR, latency, and
CRD-lite rather than treating AUROC as the only target.

\subsection{Stream Construction}
The protocol converts a static anomaly-detection test set into stream-ordered
evaluation items with a common schema:
\texttt{stream\_index}, \texttt{image\_path}, \texttt{label},
\texttt{category}, \texttt{source\_split}, and \texttt{anomaly\_type}.
For a category~\(c\), a generated stream is written as
\[
S_{c,\tau,\epsilon,r}=\{(x_t,y_t,a_t)\}_{t=1}^{T},
\]
where \(x_t\) is the image at stream index~\(t\), \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\) is the
normal/anomaly label, \(a_t\) is the anomaly type, \(\tau\in\{\mathrm{iid},
\mathrm{bursty}\}\) is the stream type, \(\epsilon\) is the contamination
setting, and \(r\) is the random seed. The i.i.d. setting samples the requested
normal/anomaly mix subject to available images. The bursty setting uses the same
item schema but places anomalous samples into one or more contiguous blocks.
Stream generation preserves label integrity and avoids duplicate image paths.
When requested stream prevalence, contamination, or burst layout cannot be
matched exactly, the generator records the applied statistics and emits a
warning rather than silently fabricating a perfect stream.

\subsection{Baseline Interface}
Each baseline consumes the same stream file and writes the same score schema:
\texttt{stream\_index}, \texttt{image\_path}, \texttt{label},
\texttt{category}, \texttt{anomaly\_score}, \texttt{latency\_ms},
\texttt{peak\_vram\_mb}, and \texttt{status}. This interface keeps the
protocol independent of a particular detector implementation. PatchCore is
treated as a training-light reference baseline, while WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and
RareCLIP are treated as CLIP-style baselines. PatchCore and RareCLIP use their
default/SCS memory setting in the main comparison; WinCLIP and
AnomalyCLIP use default/no-memory settings.

\subsection{Metrics}
For each detector~\(f\), the wrapper emits an anomaly score
\(s_t=f(x_t)\) and a measured latency for each stream item. ZIAD evaluates the
resulting score sequence along five axes. First, it compares i.i.d. streams with
bursty streams, where anomalous samples are concentrated into contiguous
blocks. Second, it evaluates contamination levels, including the compact
settings \(\epsilon \in \{0, 0.05\}\). Third, it records score
calibration, with the current main comparison using no post-hoc
calibration. Fourth, it records memory or coreset-style state where a baseline
uses it, while leaving memory-policy sweeps outside this focused slice. Fifth,
it reports latency alongside image AUROC, AUPR, ECE, and CRD-lite.

Image AUROC and AUPR are computed from \(\{(y_t,s_t)\}_{t=1}^{T}\) for each
stream. ECE is computed as a diagnostic after min--max normalizing emitted
anomaly scores, because the wrapped baselines do not all output calibrated
probabilities. Aggregate tables report means over the completed category and
seed shards for each dataset/baseline slice.

CRD-lite is a lightweight contamination robustness diagnostic implemented in
ZIAD.
For a fixed dataset, category, stream type, baseline, memory policy,
calibration, prevalence, and seed, the implementation compares a contaminated
run at~\(\epsilon\) to the matching \(\epsilon=0\) run:
\[
\mathrm{CRD\text{-}lite}(\epsilon)=
\frac{1}{2}\left[
(\mathrm{AUROC}_{0}-\mathrm{AUROC}_{\epsilon})+
(\mathrm{AUPR}_{0}-\mathrm{AUPR}_{\epsilon})
\right].
\]
Positive values indicate that the average of the AUROC and AUPR terms dropped
under contamination. Negative values are possible when the two terms move in
opposite directions or when the averaged measured metric improves. Therefore,
CRD-lite should be read together with its AUROC and AUPR terms; a negative
value is not, by itself, evidence that contamination improves robustness. This
is a signed proxy used for compact diagnostics, not a full theoretical
robustness metric.

Measurement generation is deliberately separated from claim approval: outputs

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
The current focused comparison is category-sharded. Each category shard
contains 12 metric rows: two stream types, two contamination settings, and
three seeds. MVTec AD contributes 15 categories and 180 rows per baseline.
VisA contributes 12 categories and 144 rows per baseline. All generated outputs
are kept in a dedicated paper-evaluation output root, separate from smoke and
full-P0 production-validation outputs.

PatchCore uses the documented sampler setting of 0.1. Baseline source URLs and
commit hashes are pinned in the experiment configuration;
this paper intentionally does not invent or replace them.

Runtime metadata is tracked separately in the runtime environment document. The
current audit/build environment records an Intel Core i5-14500 CPU, Ubuntu
22.04.5, Python 3.10.12, and a PyTorch 1.13.1+cu116 stack. Latency columns are
averages of wrapper-reported \texttt{latency\_ms} values emitted for metric
rows; they reflect the local wrapper timing path rather than a standardized
cross-hardware benchmark.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Result Governance}
ZIAD keeps measurement generation and claim approval separate.
A governance record tracks whether outputs have passed row-count and
category-count checks, finite metric audits, provenance review, runtime
documentation, and manual approval. These safeguards are engineering controls
for reproducibility; the paper-facing claims are limited to the audited
evaluation described above.

The near-zero \(\Delta\)B-I values in
\tabref{tab:paper-candidate-stream-epsilon-breakdown} constitute a substantive
finding: all four baselines score each image independently of stream arrival
pattern. This offline-first design is shared across CLIP-style zero-shot
detectors and the training-light PatchCore reference, and is not visible from
static benchmark evaluation; ZIAD makes it explicit through matched i.i.d.\ and
bursty stream construction. To verify that the protocol can detect order
sensitivity when it does exist, we added an intentionally order-sensitive
diagnostic probe, OrderSensitiveToy, that mixes the current image-derived score
with a short sliding window of previous scores.
\tabref{tab:order-sensitive-toy-stream-epsilon} shows non-zero \(\Delta\)B-I

positive diagnostic control rather than a statistically significant detector
comparison. The probe is not a proposed detector; it demonstrates that the
stream-order axis is observable when a scoring rule depends on previous stream
items. Future protocol slices targeting truly online detectors -- where memory
or thresholds adapt during the stream -- will require non-zero \(\Delta\)B-I to
validate online behavior.

To strengthen this control with a more realistic adaptation layer, we evaluate
a post-hoc \emph{OnlineWindow}($\alpha, W$) wrapper that consumes the same frozen
baseline scores~$s_t$ and emits an adaptive score
$\tilde{s}_t = \alpha\,s_t + (1-\alpha)\cdot \mathrm{mean}(s_{t-W:t-1})$. The
wrapper requires no new inference; it post-processes the existing score CSVs.
\tabref{tab:online-window-delta-bi} reports per-baseline \(\Delta\)B-I with
category/seed bootstrap intervals for the canonical configuration
$(\alpha{=}0.3, W{=}8)$. The frozen baselines remain stream-order invariant
($\Delta\text{B-I}\!\approx\!0$ across the board), but the OnlineWindow wrapper
yields statistically significant non-zero $\Delta$B-I in three out of eight
(baseline, dataset) cells -- VisA AnomalyCLIP, RareCLIP, and PatchCore -- with
positive point estimates in all eight cells. This is a post-hoc positive
control, not a new detector: within a burst, the sliding-window history can
reinforce anomalous scores. The result shows that ZIAD can surface order
sensitivity when a scoring rule uses previous stream items.

\section{Discussion}
The audited results illustrate why ZIAD treats the benchmark as a
multi-objective evaluation. A detector that is strong in AUROC may not be the
best calibrated or fastest method on every dataset. The protocol therefore
separates image discrimination, calibration, latency, and stream robustness
while recording memory configuration for later controlled slices. This
separation is the main contribution of the paper: the benchmark is intended to
make operational trade-offs visible for zero-shot and training-light industrial
anomaly detection. Memory policy is recorded by the execution protocol, but a
controlled memory-policy comparison is outside the main evidence slice reported
here.

Stream-order behavior decomposes into two complementary findings under ZIAD.
The four frozen baselines are order invariant: whether CLIP-style zero-shot or
training-light, each scores images independently of stream sequence. This
offline-first pattern is not exposed by static benchmark evaluation. The same
baselines, when composed with a thin post-hoc OnlineWindow($\alpha,W$) wrapper,
yield statistically significant non-zero $\Delta$B-I in three of eight
(baseline, dataset) cells, with positive point estimates in all eight cells.
Together, these two halves show that the protocol's stream-order axis is
empirically measurable, not trivially zero, and that frozen IAD detectors and
post-hoc adaptive wrappers occupy distinct, separable points on that axis.
OrderSensitiveToy is retained as a weak diagnostic; OnlineWindow is the stronger
positive control. The next required reviewer-facing experiment is a genuinely
online detector whose memory, coreset, or threshold state changes during stream
processing.

The present study has not yet elevated temperature-scaling or memory-policy
variants into the main comparison. Those axes are implemented in the
protocol and validated in auxiliary protocol checks, but the focused paper
story is the calibration-none comparison plus a clear plan for calibration and
memory-policy ablations.

\subsection{Extensions Beyond the Focused Slice}
The focused slice in this paper is intentionally narrower than the full ZIAD
proposal. The same protocol design extends along three grouped directions:
richer online policies and calibration variants, including FIFO/Reservoir-style
memory, Prototype-EMA, temperature scaling, isotonic regression, and
sliding-window reliability; broader stream and dataset coverage, including
longer epsilon/prevalence sweeps, drift, chronological streams, and additional
industrial datasets; and stronger analysis layers, including statistical
uncertainty estimates, mechanistic intervention studies, and compute or energy
disclosure. These axes are part of the protocol design but remain outside the
current focused evaluation.

\section{Limitations}
The present study has five important limitations. First, it reports a focused
evaluation slice rather than an exhaustive benchmark. Second, the focused
setting uses stream length 64; longer streams may be needed before stronger
deployment claims are made. Third, latency values depend on the local software
and hardware path and are not a controlled cross-hardware benchmark. Fourth,
calibration and memory-policy variants are not yet part of the main comparison,
so the paper avoids broad claims about temperature scaling or online memory
until those slices are reviewed. Fifth, the stream-order invariance finding
applies to the four evaluated baselines; truly online detectors that update
their models during streaming may exhibit non-zero \(\Delta\)B-I, and the
current results do not characterize that regime. The order-sensitive toy probe
is only a diagnostic control, not a proposed detector. Negative CRD-lite
values are interpreted through their AUROC/AUPR component terms and confidence
intervals rather than as robustness gains.

\subsection{Stream-Length Sanity Check}
We include a tiny stream-length sensitivity sanity check for MVTec AD bottle
with WinCLIP at stream lengths 64, 128, and 256. These measured rows are
preliminary appendix evidence only; they do not support a broad stream-length
robustness claim without additional categories, additional baselines, and
manual review.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\paragraph{Construct validity.}
ZIAD measures stream order, burstiness, contamination, calibration, and latency
through practical proxies, and records memory configuration for future
controlled sweeps. CRD-lite is intentionally a compact signed diagnostic rather
than a complete robustness theory, and ECE is computed after score normalization
because wrapped baselines do not share calibrated probability outputs.

We also ran a preliminary strong-contamination diagnostic for
AnomalyCLIP on a completed subset of VisA categories with
\(\epsilon \in \{0,0.05,0.1,0.2\}\). As shown in
\tabref{tab:strong-epsilon-completed-diagnostic}, this CPU-bound diagnostic
completed six categories. AUROC degrades at stronger contamination in the
candle slice, but AUPR can move in the opposite direction, so the signed
CRD-lite average does not become uniformly positive or monotonic. At
\(\epsilon=0.2\), for example, the candle diagnostic has an AUROC term of
\(+0.049\) and an AUPR term of \(-0.107\); at \(\epsilon=0.05\), capsules has
a positive CRD-lite value of \(+0.031\). We therefore treat the mixed signs as
category-dependent diagnostic behavior driven by the AUROC/AUPR terms, not as a
main result or as evidence that stronger contamination fully resolves CRD-lite
ambiguity.

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
The protocol enforces no duplicate stream images, label integrity, row-count
checks, development-time result governance, and category-sharded resume
behavior. However, wrapper differences, local dependency versions, and
model-specific preprocessing can still affect measured scores and timing.

\paragraph{External validity.}
The main comparison covers MVTec AD and VisA with four representative
baselines, but it is not an exhaustive industrial deployment study. The tiny
stream-length sanity check is especially narrow: one dataset category and one
baseline, with no claim that the trend generalizes.

\paragraph{Runtime validity.}
Latency is local-runtime dependent. The current runtime documentation records
the audit environment and the paper reports wrapper-emitted timing values, but
the wrappers are not a controlled systems benchmark: device path, model-loading
scope, batching behavior, and timing granularity can differ across baselines.

\section{Conclusion}
ZIAD reframes industrial anomaly detection evaluation around ordered streams
instead of unordered test sets. The protocol makes stream construction,
contamination, calibration, latency, memory configuration, and restartable
execution explicit while preserving compatibility with established industrial
datasets and representative zero-shot or training-light baselines. The current
comparison across MVTec AD and VisA shows that accuracy, calibration, and
latency expose different operational views of the same detectors, motivating
multi-metric reporting rather than AUROC-only leaderboards. Broader
stream-length, calibration, and memory-policy conclusions require additional
reviewed slices. By making these axes explicit, ZIAD offers a reproducible path
for evaluating industrial anomaly detectors under streaming conditions.
