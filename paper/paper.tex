\begin{abstract}
Industrial anomaly detection is commonly evaluated on static image sets, yet
inspection systems receive ordered samples and must handle contamination,
reliability, and latency. We present ZIAD, a streaming
evaluation protocol for industrial anomaly detection. ZIAD converts static
benchmarks into controlled i.i.d.\ and bursty streams, executes
category-sharded evaluations, and reports discrimination, ECE, latency,
CRD-lite, and stream-order sensitivity. On MVTec AD and VisA with PatchCore,
WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP, ZIAD yields three observations. First, the
four main baselines are nearly order invariant, making an implicit property of
static scoring measurable. Second, state-updating diagnostic memory-bank
detectors produce statistically significant non-zero $\Delta$B-I, showing that
the protocol detects order-sensitive adaptation when it is present. Third,
accuracy, ECE, and latency rankings differ within datasets, so
AUROC-only reporting hides operational trade-offs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Industrial anomaly detection is often evaluated on static test sets, but
practical inspection systems receive images over time. This mismatch matters
for zero-shot and training-light detectors, where deployment depends on
latency, score calibration, stream order, anomaly prevalence, and
contamination as well as ranking accuracy. We call this protocol ZIAD,
shorthand for a streaming evaluation framework targeting zero-shot and
training-light industrial anomaly detection. It converts static benchmarks
into controlled streams and reports detectors along five diagnostics:
discrimination accuracy (AUROC, AUPR), score reliability (ECE), latency,
contamination sensitivity (CRD-lite), and stream-order sensitivity
($\Delta$B-I, the bursty-minus-i.i.d.\ AUROC shift). The protocol also records
baseline state and memory metadata, with memory-policy sweeps left to future
work. PatchCore provides a training-light reference; WinCLIP,
AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP represent CLIP-style zero-shot baselines.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a streaming industrial anomaly detection protocol that turns static
category benchmarks into matched i.i.d.\ and bursty streams for zero-shot and
training-light detectors;
\item a reproducible category-sharded execution design with provenance and
row-count checks;
\item a common baseline interface and multi-metric comparison across MVTec AD
and VisA with PatchCore, WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP;
\item an accuracy--latency--reliability analysis that exposes operational
trade-offs hidden by static AUROC-only reporting; and
\item a stream-order characterization showing measured order-invariance for the
four main baselines and measurable non-zero $\Delta$B-I for state-updating
positive controls.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Industrial anomaly detection and its benchmarks.}
Industrial anomaly detection benchmarks have typically measured whether a method
can separate normal product images from defective ones on static category-level
test sets. MVTec AD~\cite{Bergmann2019MVTec} and VisA~\cite{Zou2022VisA} served
as central benchmarks, and training-light methods such as
PatchCore~\cite{Roth2022PatchCore} became common reference points. Recent studies
argued that a single AUROC number was insufficient: standardized suites such as
IM-IAD~\cite{Xie2024IMIAD} and critical analyses of the academia--industry
gap~\cite{Baitieva2025Beyond} reported multiple metrics and stressed deployment
realism. However, even these comprehensive multi-metric studies evaluated on
static, unordered test sets and did not report score calibration or distinguish
i.i.d. from bursty arrivals. ZIAD instead reorders the same datasets into streams
and adds ECE and arrival pattern as first-class evaluation diagnostics, making
assumptions that IM-IAD held fixed into observable, configurable protocol
dimensions.

\paragraph{Zero-shot anomaly detection and CLIP-style baselines.}
CLIP-style detectors reduced category-specific supervision with
vision-language representations or prompt-driven scoring. WinCLIP,
AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP are used here as representative
\cite{Jeong2023WinCLIP,Zhou2024AnomalyCLIP,He2025RareCLIP}. Notably, recent
industrial benchmarks explicitly excluded such zero-shot vision-language models
from their scope~\cite{Baitieva2025Beyond}, leaving their streaming behavior
unexamined. This paper does not modify their architectures; it evaluates how
their outputs behave under streaming conditions. PatchCore is included separately
as a training-light reference rather than a CLIP-based method.

\paragraph{Streaming and online anomaly evaluation.}
Streaming evaluation emphasizes sample order, distribution shift, bursty arrival,
contamination, and resume-safe execution. General frameworks for time-series and
tabular streams reported that no single detector dominates across stream
conditions~\cite{Calikus2020NoFreeLunch,Chandola2009AnomalyDetection,Bifet2010MOA}.
These concerns were far less visible in static industrial image leaderboards. ZIAD
ports this streaming view to industrial images, making i.i.d. and bursty arrivals
comparable under the same baselines and metrics.

\paragraph{Calibration and reliability.}
Anomaly scores often drive thresholds or alarms, so ranking quality alone is
insufficient. ECE~\cite{Guo2017Calibration} quantifies whether scores are
interpretable across streams and contamination settings. ZIAD reports ECE
alongside AUROC, AUPR, latency, and CRD-lite.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\figref{fig:ziad-protocol-overview} summarizes the focused ZIAD flow. The
figure is a protocol overview, not a result: static industrial datasets are
converted into stream-ordered evaluation items, passed through shared baseline
interfaces, optionally varied along protocol axes, and summarized with metrics
plus category-sharded audit checks.

ZIAD follows four design principles: controlled stream construction, matched
stream/contamination/category/seed comparisons, category/seed-stratified
aggregation, and restartable evidence generation with provenance checks. These
principles make order and contamination effects measurable rather than assumed.

\subsection{Stream Construction}
The protocol converts a static anomaly-detection test set into stream-ordered
evaluation items with a common schema:
\texttt{stream\_index}, \texttt{image\_path}, \texttt{label},
\texttt{category}, \texttt{source\_split}, and \texttt{anomaly\_type}.
For a category~\(c\), a generated stream is written as
\[
S_{c,\tau,\epsilon,r}=\{(x_t,y_t,a_t)\}_{t=1}^{T},
\]
where \(x_t\) is the image at stream index~\(t\), \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\) is the
normal/anomaly label, \(a_t\) is the anomaly type, \(\tau\in\{\mathrm{iid},
\mathrm{bursty}\}\) is the stream type, \(\epsilon\) is the contamination
setting, and \(r\) is the random seed. The i.i.d. setting samples the requested
normal/anomaly mix subject to available images. The bursty setting uses the same
item schema but places anomalous samples into one or more contiguous blocks.
Stream generation preserves label integrity and avoids duplicate image paths.
When requested stream prevalence, contamination, or burst layout cannot be
matched exactly, the generator records the applied statistics and emits a
warning rather than silently fabricating a perfect stream.

\subsection{Baseline Interface}
Each baseline consumes the same stream file and writes the same score schema:
\texttt{stream\_index}, \texttt{image\_path}, \texttt{label},
\texttt{category}, \texttt{anomaly\_score}, \texttt{latency\_ms},
\texttt{peak\_vram\_mb}, and \texttt{status}. This interface keeps the
protocol independent of a particular detector implementation. PatchCore is
treated as a training-light reference baseline, while WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and
RareCLIP are treated as CLIP-style baselines. PatchCore uses its default fixed coreset; RareCLIP runs with its default SCS
policy (RareCLIP's default memory-update policy), which actively updates
patch-feature and image-feature memory per sample; WinCLIP and AnomalyCLIP use stateless inference.

\subsection{Metrics}
For each detector~\(f\), the wrapper emits an anomaly score
\(s_t=f(x_t)\) and a measured latency for each stream item. ZIAD evaluates the
resulting score sequence along five diagnostics. First, it compares i.i.d. streams with
bursty streams, where anomalous samples are concentrated into contiguous
blocks. Second, it evaluates contamination levels, including the compact
settings \(\epsilon \in \{0, 0.05\}\). Third, it records score
reliability through ECE, with the current main comparison using no post-hoc
calibration transform. Fourth, it records memory or coreset-style state where a
baseline uses it (memory-policy sweeps are future work). Fifth,
it reports latency alongside image AUROC, AUPR, ECE, and CRD-lite.

Image AUROC and AUPR are computed from \(\{(y_t,s_t)\}_{t=1}^{T}\) for each
stream. ECE is computed as a diagnostic after min--max normalizing emitted
anomaly scores, because the wrapped baselines do not all output calibrated
probabilities. Aggregate tables report means over the completed category and
seed shards for each dataset/baseline slice.

CRD-lite is a signed contamination robustness proxy. For matched
$\epsilon$-vs-$\epsilon{=}0$ run pairs:
\[
\mathrm{CRD\text{-}lite}(\epsilon)=
\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[
(\mathrm{AUROC}_{0}-\mathrm{AUROC}_{\epsilon})+
(\mathrm{AUPR}_{0}-\mathrm{AUPR}_{\epsilon})
\bigr].
\]
Positive values indicate average discrimination degraded under contamination;
negative values are not interpreted as a robustness gain by themselves; they
indicate that the AUROC and AUPR terms moved in opposite directions or that the
AUPR term dominated the signed average, so the component metrics must be read
alongside CRD-lite.

\subsection{Category-Sharded Execution}
The focused evaluation covers MVTec AD and VisA, the baselines
PatchCore, WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and RareCLIP, stream length~64, and seeds
\(\{0,1,2\}\). Execution is category-sharded so long-running steps can resume
without confusing a completed category shard with a full paper result.
Measurement generation is deliberately separated from claim approval: outputs
are reviewed through row-count, category-count, metric-validity, provenance,
sampler-setting, runtime, and manual checks before being used for stronger
claims.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
The current focused comparison is category-sharded. Each category shard
contains 12 metric rows: two stream types, two contamination settings, and
three seeds. MVTec AD contributes 15 categories and 180 rows per baseline.
VisA contributes 12 categories and 144 rows per baseline.

Two distinct configurations appear in this paper and should not be conflated.
(a)~The \emph{focused baseline comparison} (\tabref{tab:paper-candidate-baseline-comparison}
and \tabref{tab:paper-candidate-stream-epsilon-breakdown})
uses stream length~64 with seeds~$\{0,1,2\}$ across all categories of both
datasets. (b)~The \emph{stream-order detectability slice} (state-updating
diagnostic detectors and the RareCLIP replication, \secref{sec:stream-contamination})
uses MVTec AD bottle, cable, and capsule with a requested length of~256 capped
to the full available test set (applied~83 samples per run; see below).
OnlineWindowKNN (OWK) and OnlinePatchCoreLite (OPCLite) use seeds~0--9 and
$\epsilon \in \{0,0.05\}$ (60 bootstrap strata each, i.e.\ category/seed strata); PrototypeEMA used a
pilot run with seeds~0--4 (30 strata); the RareCLIP corroborating replication
uses seeds~0--9 and $\epsilon{=}0$ (30 strata). The wider seed range and
full-test-set length are chosen to give the positive control sufficient power.

PatchCore uses sampler percentage 0.1; baseline source commits are pinned in
the experiment configuration.

Runtime metadata is tracked separately in the runtime environment document. The
current audit/build environment records an Intel Core i5-14500 CPU, Ubuntu
22.04.5, Python 3.10.12, and a PyTorch 1.13.1+cu116 stack. Latency columns are
averages of wrapper-reported \texttt{latency\_ms} values emitted for metric
rows; they reflect the local wrapper timing path rather than a standardized
cross-hardware benchmark. Baselines are run from pinned source commits; stream
generation preserves label integrity and records applied statistics.

\section{Results}
The results are organized around three questions that static industrial anomaly
detection tables do not answer directly. First, how do representative
zero-shot and training-light baselines trade off AUROC, AUPR, ECE, and
latency? Second, do matched i.i.d.\ and bursty streams reveal stream-order
sensitivity? Third, does the $\Delta$B-I estimator respond when a detector's
state actually changes online? The focused comparison answers the first
question on MVTec AD and VisA, while the stream-order detectability slice
answers the latter two with controlled positive probes.

\tabref{tab:paper-candidate-baseline-comparison} summarizes the current
focused comparison for the calibration-none setting (the no-post-hoc-calibration
configuration of the Metrics section). PatchCore has the
highest mean AUROC and AUPR on both datasets; ECE and latency leaders differ
by dataset. ECE values are not directly comparable across datasets because
scores are min--max normalized per baseline before computing ECE; calibration
diagnostics are therefore read \emph{within} a dataset rather than across
datasets. These observations support the protocol analysis without asserting a
universal detector ranking.

The near-zero $\Delta$B-I values in
\tabref{tab:paper-candidate-stream-epsilon-breakdown} are a \emph{baseline
characterization}. WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and PatchCore are stateless
(order-invariance expected). RareCLIP updates memory per sample yet also
yields near-zero $\Delta$B-I under its default SCS policy on the evaluated
slice. ZIAD therefore separates two behaviors that a static benchmark would
merge: frozen scoring is empirically order invariant, and a state-updating
method may or may not translate its internal updates into order-sensitive
scores.

Two additional probes (an OrderSensitiveToy score-mixing probe and a post-hoc
OnlineWindow wrapper) were also run but are underpowered and inconclusive:
point estimates are directionally positive yet their bootstrap intervals
largely include zero. We report them only for completeness; the full

A corroborating replication (configuration~(b); see \secref{sec:setup})
confirms the RareCLIP finding where the estimator is known to detect order
effects: OnlinePatchCoreLite yields $\Delta\text{B-I} = +0.014$
(95\% CI $[{+0.002},{+0.027}]$, excludes zero), while RareCLIP with default
SCS memory yields $\Delta\text{B-I} = 0.000$
(95\% CI $[{-0.006},{+0.006}]$, includes zero).
Effects of magnitude $|\delta| \geq 0.006$ are excluded for RareCLIP at
full-test-set length; the near-zero result is not attributable to insufficient
stream length but to the absence of a detectable order effect under SCS
diversity sampling.

These positive controls validate the computational path of the $\Delta$B-I
estimator. They are not proposed leaderboard methods; their role is to show
that ZIAD surfaces order sensitivity when online state changes affect future
scores.

\section{Discussion}
For practitioners, the value of separating these axes is operational: a
deployment can pick an operating point on the accuracy--latency--reliability
surface rather than inheriting whichever detector tops a static AUROC
leaderboard, and can treat order-invariance as a property to be verified for a
given operating mode rather than assumed.
Operationally, no measurable AUROC difference between i.i.d.\ and bursty
arrivals was observed for the four main baselines on the evaluated slice.
This should not be read as evidence that stream order is irrelevant; rather,
it identifies which evaluated modes are effectively order invariant. The
state-updating positive controls then demonstrate the complementary case:
when online memory changes affect future scores, ZIAD reports non-zero
$\Delta$B-I.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The study has the following scope limitations.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Focused slice and length bound.} The focused baseline comparison
uses stream length~64 with seeds~$\{0,1,2\}$ on MVTec AD and VisA. Longer
single-category streams are not merely undone but structurally bounded by
dataset size: no category in either benchmark has more than 201 test images
(MVTec AD peaks at 167; VisA at 201), so a requested length of~256 is always
capped to the full per-category test set. Stream-length conclusions are
therefore limited to the achievable range, and broader claims would require
either multi-category concatenation or additional datasets with larger
per-category test sets.

\item \textbf{Baseline scope.} WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP, and PatchCore run in
stateless inference mode; their near-zero $\Delta$B-I characterises this
configuration and does not generalise to state-updating modes. RareCLIP runs
with active SCS memory updates by default yet still produces near-zero
$\Delta$B-I on the focused MVTec AD slice (the with-CI result is given in
\secref{sec:stream-contamination}); this finding is specific to SCS diversity
sampling and the evaluated categories and lengths.

\item \textbf{Positive controls.} OWK and OPCLite results are reported in
\tabref{tab:online-memory-detector-comparison}; the detailed per-length
breakdown is in the generated JSON artifacts.
The positive-control effect size of OPCLite is slice-dependent: on VisA PCB
categories (201 test images each), OPCLite does not reach significance at
either length, so the detectability boundary cannot be established there.
The near-zero result for RareCLIP is therefore conditioned on the MVTec AD
bottle/cable/capsule slice where the positive control is itself significant.
These probes validate the $\Delta$B-I estimator's computational path but do
not establish that all online deployed methods will produce detectable effects
at either scale.

\item \textbf{Latency.} Latency values are local-runtime averages that scale
with memory-bank size and dataset; cross-dataset comparisons are not
method-level rankings.

\item \textbf{Calibration and memory.} Temperature scaling and
memory-policy sweeps are implemented in the protocol but are outside the main
comparison reported here.

\item \textbf{CRD-lite.} CRD-lite is a signed proxy, not a standalone
robustness score, and its negative values must be read with the component
AUROC and AUPR terms as defined in the Metrics section. A preliminary
strong-$\epsilon$ diagnostic for AnomalyCLIP is available in the generated
artifacts and is not analysed further here.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
Protocol integrity is maintained through no-duplicate stream construction,
label integrity, row-count checks, and category-sharded resume behavior.
Wrapper differences, dependency versions, and preprocessing can still affect
scores and timing; latency values are not a controlled cross-hardware
benchmark.

\section{Conclusion}
ZIAD turns stream order, burstiness, contamination, score reliability, and
latency into auditable diagnostics rather than implicit assumptions of static
benchmarks. This focused study leaves broader calibration and memory-policy
sweeps to future work, while providing a category-sharded, provenance-checked
path for reproducibly evaluating industrial anomaly detectors under streaming
conditions.
